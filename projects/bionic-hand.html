A Bionic Hand India Could Actually Afford

Advanced bionic hands sold for USD 30,000–60,000. Most amputees in India wore a plastic cosmetic hand instead. We set out to build a multi-grip bionic hand priced under USD 3,000 — and when the parts we needed didn't exist at that price, we built them.

01 — Snapshot

Engineering Snapshot.

Challenge

Deliver bionic-hand function — multiple grips, real load capacity, muscle-signal control — at a price an Indian family could realistically reach. Off-the-shelf actuators, sensors and electronics either didn't fit the hand or didn't fit the budget.

My Role

Core member of the R&D mechanical team, working at the mechanical core of the hand: the custom linear actuators and the patented thumb swivel. I moved production parts to HP Multi Jet Fusion, failure-tested every design iteration, and built the in-house test strategy that the certification lab testing was based on.

Systems

Custom linear actuators · patented thumb swivel with position feedback · in-house EMG sensors · custom PCBs, power circuits and battery packs · Bluetooth app control · MJF-printed structure.

Outcome

KalArm launched within budget at under USD 3,000, with 18 preset grips plus 6 custom, ~8 kg load capacity, and EMG + app control — and went into production. It is still on sale today.

02 — The Story

Engineered to a Price.

Step 01 — The Problem

The problem wasn't technology. It was price.

Bionic hands already existed. Multi-grip myoelectric hands sold for USD 30,000–60,000 in Western markets, and even the most affordable 3D-printed option cost around USD 13,000. For most upper-limb amputees in India, that put a functional hand out of reach entirely. The realistic option was a plastic cosmetic prosthesis — it looks like a hand, but it doesn't grip, hold or work like one.

So we set a hard target: a hand that competes on function with the bionic hands on the market, at under USD 3,000. Not cheap for the sake of cheap — a price that was at least within reach. Every engineering decision after that was measured against it.

Step 02 — Actuation

No actuator on the market fit. So we built our own.

The first wall was actuation. We needed enough force to grip and hold real objects, inside the envelope of a human finger, at a cost that worked inside a sub-USD 3,000 product. Off-the-shelf actuators gave us two of those three at best: small ones lacked the force, strong ones didn't fit, and the ones that did both were priced for a USD 50,000 hand.

So we designed the linear actuators from scratch around our own force, size and cost requirements. The internal design is patent- and NDA-protected, so I won't detail it here — but owning the actuator meant we owned the trade-offs instead of inheriting someone else's.

Step 03 — Thumb

A thumb that knows where it is.

A hand that can only open and close isn't much of a hand. Most useful grips — key grip, tripod, pinch — depend on where the thumb sits. We designed a thumb swivel mechanism with position feedback, so the controller knows the thumb's position and can switch grips reliably. That mechanism is protected by Indian Patent 403148.

Step 04 — Electronics

Sensors and electronics, built to our spec.

The same story repeated for electronics. The EMG sensors that read muscle signals were either unreliable or too expensive for our price point, so we developed our own to our requirements. We built our own PCBs and power circuits, and customised the battery packs to fit the hand.

Step 05 — Manufacturing

Taking machining out of the cost.

Machined parts were one of the biggest costs in the build. We moved to rapid prototyping for development, and I took production parts to HP Multi Jet Fusion. MJF gave us commercial-grade, strong, repeatable parts without machining set-ups for every revision — which cut a large share of the cost and let a design change go from CAD to a usable part in days instead of weeks.

Step 06 — Integration

Many workstreams, one product.

We were a small team, so we ran actuators, thumb, sensors, electronics and structure as parallel streams that converged on each prototype, instead of finishing one before starting the next. It was messier than a linear plan, but it's why we moved as fast as we did. I tested each design iteration to find its failure points — combining analysis with physical testing — before the next round, so each prototype fixed real problems rather than guessed ones.

Step 07 — Compliance

Learning ISO 13485 from zero.

None of us had taken a medical device to market before. We learned ISO 13485 on the go — reading the standards, working out what they actually required of our design and processes, and changing the product to meet them. I built in-house functional and durability tests that mirrored what the certification labs would check. Those tests became the basis for the external lab testing, and I worked with the testing body through the process — which saved time and money compared with discovering failures at the lab.

04 — Takeaways

What This Proves.

When the part you need doesn't exist at the price you need, waiting for the market isn't a plan. This project was a chain of those decisions — actuators, thumb, sensors, electronics, manufacturing — each one made against a single number: under USD 3,000. It's the same discipline I bring to stuck hardware now: find the constraint that actually matters, and engineer to it instead of around it.

What I'd do next
  • Two things. First, publish independent user-outcome data — grip success rates and daily-use time from real users — because a spec sheet proves capability, not adoption.
  • Second, design for serviceability in the field: in a market like India, the cost of a repair trip can matter as much as the price of the hand.
